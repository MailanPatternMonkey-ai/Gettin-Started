## What a Novikov algebra is

A vector space with a bilinear product \(\circ\) is Novikov if it satisfies

**right commutativity**
\[
(x\circ y)\circ z=(x\circ z)\circ y
\]

**left-symmetric associators**
\[
(x,y,z)=(y,x,z),\qquad
(x,y,z):=(x\circ y)\circ z-x\circ(y\circ z).
\]

Equivalently: right multiplications commute, and the associator is symmetric in the first two slots. It is not associative in general. Every Novikov algebra is Lie-admissible: the commutator
\[
[x,y]:=x\circ y-y\circ x
\]
is a Lie bracket. That is the start of the Gelfand–Dorfman picture.

Convention note: some papers call this *left* Novikov and some *right* Novikov, depending on whether they put the derivation on the left or the right factor. Your text says **opposite Novikov products**, so both orientations are in play.

## Gelfand’s realization (the one that actually appears)

Let \(A\) be commutative and associative, and let \(\partial\) be a derivation of \(A\). Then

\[
x\triangleright y:=x\cdot\partial y
\]

is left Novikov, and the opposite product

\[
x\triangleleft y:=(\partial x)\cdot y
\]

is right Novikov. Both identities follow from the Leibniz rule and from \(A\) being commutative. This is the original Gelfand observation inside Gelfand–Dorfman (1979); Balinskii–Novikov found the same identities from hydrodynamic Poisson brackets.

On your phase circle the obvious choice is

\[
A=C^\infty(S^1),\qquad
\partial=\frac{d}{d\theta}
\]

or the same operator in the residual coordinate \(\rho\). Then, for smooth functions \(f,g\),

\[
(f\triangleright g)(\theta)=f(\theta)\,g'(\theta),\qquad
(f\triangleleft g)(\theta)=f'(\theta)\,g(\theta).
\]

That is the **Gelfand–Dorfman realization** named in the tree: Novikov structure realized by “multiply, then differentiate,” not by some new binary law on bins.

Quick check of right commutativity for \(\triangleright\):

\[
(f\triangleright g)\triangleright h=fg'h',\qquad
(f\triangleright h)\triangleright g=fh'g'.
\]

Those agree because ordinary multiplication of functions commutes. The associator becomes \(fg''h'\), which is symmetric in \(f,g\).

## Why “opposite,” and why the naive product fails

You already have three multiplications on the same vector space of phase functions:

| Product | Formula | Type |
|---|---|---|
| pointwise | \(fg\) | commutative associative |
| left Novikov | \(f\,\partial g\) | Novikov |
| right Novikov | \((\partial f)\,g\) | opposite Novikov |

The naive move is to treat “just multiply the functions” as if it were compatible with \(\circ\) on the whole noncommutative package (Novikov product + pointwise product + composition of operators). It is not.

- Pointwise product is commutative; Novikov product is not: \(f\triangleright g-g\triangleright f=fg'-gf'\), the Wronskian.
- The Novikov product is not a derivation of the pointwise product in both slots.
- If you pass to operators (left/right multiplications, or to a larger algebra generated by both products), the naive formula \((fg)\circ h=f(g\circ h)\) fails in general.

That is what “the naive product fails on the full noncommutative algebra” is saying. The nested phase algebras in v2.0 §6 already warn that **conditional averaging is not multiplication-preserving**. Same issue: reducing from \(\theta\) to \(z\) to \(\rho\) preserves the *observation*, not the *algebra product*.

## Gelfand–Dorfman algebra, not just Novikov

A **Gelfand–Dorfman algebra** (GD-algebra) is a space with two operations \((\circ,[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])\) such that

1. \((A,\circ)\) is Novikov,
2. \((A,[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])\) is Lie,
3. they satisfy the compatibility identity
\[
[x,y\circ z]-[z,y\circ x]+[y,x]\circ z-[y,z]\circ x-y\circ[x,z]=0.
\]

Two standard constructions:

- From any Novikov product, set \([x,y]=x\circ y-y\circ x\). The result is always a GD-algebra. In the function realization that Lie bracket is
  \[
  [f,g]=fg'-gf'.
  \]
- From a Poisson algebra with a derivation \(d\), set \(x\circ y=x\,d(y)\) and keep the Poisson bracket. Those GD-algebras are called **special**. Not every abstract GD-algebra embeds this way.

On \(C^\infty(S^1)\) the Lie bracket \([f,g]=fg'-gf'\) is the ordinary Wronskian. Restrict to Fourier modes or to vector fields and you are one step from the Witt algebra
\[
[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n},
\]
which is exactly the next item in your tree: **Witt action + cocycle**. The Virasoro cocycle is an extra central term on that Lie algebra of vector fields / densities; v2.0 §6 says the density cocycle maps to the scalar Virasoro class, and explicitly does **not** assign a physical central charge to the screen.

## 1. Central extension of Witt: the ordinary Virasoro algebra

On the phase functions you already have the centerless Witt algebra

\[
L_n=-e_n^h D_h,\qquad
[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}.
\]

Time acts by a character: \(K_F L_n K_F^{-1}=e^{2\pi i n\alpha}L_n\). On Fourier modes, \(L_0 e_j^h=-j e_j^h\), so the spectrum is all of \(\mathbb Z\). That representation is not a highest-weight module and is not a vertex algebra.

A 2-cocycle with trivial coefficients is

\[
\omega(L_m,L_n)=\frac{m^3-m}{12}\delta_{m+n,0}
=\frac1{12}I_h\bigl(((D_h^3-D_h)f)g\bigr)
\]

when \(L_m=f D_h\), \(L_n=g D_h\). It is antisymmetric by integration by parts. The cyclic identity is the polynomial identity for \(c(q)=(q^3-q)/12\). It is not a coboundary: \(b([L_1,L_{-1}])\) would force \(b(L_0)=0\), while \(b([L_2,L_{-2}])\) would force \(4b(L_0)=1/2\).

The central extension is then a choice of scalar \(\kappa\):

\[

\qquad [C,L_n]=0.
\]

The differential-operator realization on \(B_h\) has \(\kappa=0\). Your documents refuse to set \(\kappa\) from \(\alpha\), from 39, from slips, or from occupation. That refusal is correct: \(\kappa\) is extra data.

## 2. Non-central extensions: vector fields plus densities

A tensor density of weight \(\lambda\) transforms as

\[
\rho_\lambda(f)a=f D_h a+\lambda(D_h f)a.
\]

That is a Lie module \(\mathcal F_\lambda\). Pairing \(\langle a,b\rangle=I(ab)\) identifies the regular dual of \(\mathcal F_{-1}\) (vector fields) with \(\mathcal F_2\) (quadratic differentials), not with \(\mathcal F_1\). That is the Kirillov–Segal picture of the coadjoint Virasoro orbit as Sturm–Liouville operators.

Ovsienko–Roger classified \(H^2(\mathrm{Vect}(S^1),\mathcal F_\lambda)\). It is nonzero only for

\[
\lambda\in\{0,1,2,5,7\}
\]

(dim 2 for \(0,1,2\); dim 1 for \(5,7\)). Your receipt keeps five explicit low-weight representatives:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\overline c_\lambda(f,g)&=f D^{\lambda+1}g-g D^{\lambda+1}f,&&\lambda=0,1,2,\\
c_\lambda(f,g)&=(Df)D^{\lambda+1}g-(Dg)D^{\lambda+1}f,&&\lambda=1,2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Each output lives in \(\mathcal F_\lambda\). The corresponding extension is

\[
[(f,a),(g,b)]=\bigl([f,g]_D,\;
\rho_\lambda(f)b-\rho_\lambda(g)a+c(f,g)\bigr).
\]

The density summand is an **abelian ideal**, not a center, unless the module action \(\rho_\lambda\) is trivial. So these are Virasoro-type extensions, not the Virasoro algebra itself. There are 7 nontrivial Lie-algebra extensions of \(\mathrm{Vect}(S^1)\) by some \(\mathcal F_\lambda\), and 4 group-level extensions of \(\mathrm{Diff}^+(S^1)\). Analogous counts hold after you already centrally extend to Virasoro.

v1.61 also notes: vanishing of some cohomology does not kill the ordinary Virasoro class. Projection \(\mathrm{Vect}\ltimes\mathbb C\to\mathrm{Vect}\) has a Lie section, so the central class survives.

Strong lumpability is the precise stochastic version of “same visible label ⇒ same next visible law.” That is the test your geometry and TOH files keep writing. It is a property of a **kernel plus a partition**, not of a short trace.

## The condition

Let \(X_n\) be Markov on \(X\) with kernel \(P\), and let \(B:X\to Y\) be an observation (equivalently, a partition of \(X\) into fibers \(B^{-1}(y)\)). Write

\[
P_B(x,A):=P\bigl(x,B^{-1}(A)\bigr)
\]

for the law of the next observation starting from \(x\).

**Strong lumpability** (Kemeny–Snell): \(B(X_n)\) is Markov **for every initial law** if and only if

\[
B(x)=B(y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_B(x,\,\cdot\,)=P_B(y,\,\cdot\,).
\]

On a finite space, if the blocks are \(L_r\), this is: for every pair of blocks \(L_r,L_s\),

\[
\lambda_{r s}:=\sum_{j\in L_s}P(i,j)
\]

is the **same number for every** \(i\in L_r\). That common value is the lumped transition \(\Lambda_{rs}\).

In matrix form, if \(V\) is the 0–1 membership matrix of blocks and \(U\) averages (or picks representatives) inside blocks, strong lumpability is

\[
PV=V\Lambda\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad VUPV=PV.
\]

Rows of \(P\) that sit in the same block must have identical block-exit totals. Not similar. Identical.

Deterministic case: \(P(x,\,\cdot\,)=\delta_{Fx}\). Then the condition collapses to your predictive closure

\[
B(x)=B(y)\ \Longrightarrow\ B(Fx)=B(Fy).
\]

So Twin Timelines is a failure of strong lumpability for “finite slip history” as \(B\).

## Strong versus weak

| | Strong | Weak |
|---|---|---|
| Requirement | lumped process is Markov **from every start** | Markov only from **some** initial laws |
| Test | exit probabilities constant on each block | a linear system for those starts; often the stationary law works when the strong test fails |
| Implies the other | strong \(\Rightarrow\) weak | weak \(\not\Rightarrow\) strong |

Your files already flag this. A special stationary initialization can give a Markov projection without the universal criterion. Finite trace agreement establishes neither. That is Geiger–Temmel’s distinction between lumping and higher-order lumpability.

Kemeny–Snell also separate two weaker matrix conditions that suffice for *weak* lumpability: \(VUPV=PV\) (the strong one) versus a condition on the reverse chain / “past beyond the last lump gives no extra information.” Do not mix those.

## What the condition does *not* say

- It does not say the lumped chain has the same mixing time.
- It does not say means of next observables agree. Full **laws** on the next label must agree.
- It does not pass automatically to \(P^k\). Failure for \(P\) need not be failure for \(P^k\); your receipt notes that address-dependence.
- It is not proved by an empirical one-step table. A table is a lag description. Strong lumpability is an identity on the kernel.
- Passing a sector screen is not lumpability of the sector. That was the Q2 lesson: all eight parity values were visited, signed winding was not well mixed. Parity is a coarser \(B\).

## How to test it, cleanly

For a proposed \(B\) and a declared kernel \(P\):

1. List the fibers \(B^{-1}(y)\).
2. For each fiber, compute \(P_B(x,\,\cdot\,)\) at every \(x\) in the fiber (or a generating set).
3. They must be identical as measures, not close in a plot.
4. If the space is continuous, the identity has to hold almost everywhere on each fiber, for the measure class you actually use.
5. If you only have samples: you can **falsify** strong lumpability (two exact same-label states with different next-label frequencies, with a proper conditional test). You cannot establish it from a short run.

A \(\chi^2\) test of lumpability exists for finite chains (compare within-block exit counts). That tests a model against data. It does not replace the identity \(PV=V\Lambda\).

If strong lumpability fails, the coarsest repair is the Nerode construction you already wrote: identify \(x\sim y\) only when the entire future observation sequences (or future observation laws) agree. ## Definition

Let \(X_n\) be Markov with kernel \(P\), and \(Y_n=B(X_n)\) the lump (here \(B(X)=q\)).

**Weak lumpability** with respect to \(B\) and an initial law \(\alpha\): the process \(Y\) is a homogeneous Markov chain when \(X_0\sim\alpha\).

Kemeny–Snell’s criterion: fold the observed lump history back into a posterior \(\beta\) on the hidden state. Then \(Y\) is Markov under \(\alpha\) iff, for every lump \(L\),

\[
\mathbb P_\beta(Y_1=m)
\]

is the **same number for every posterior \(\beta\) that \(\alpha\) can actually reach** on the event “current lump is \(L\)”. That common number is \(\Lambda_{Lm}\).

Strong lumpability is the special case where this holds for **every** \(\alpha\). Strong \(\Rightarrow\) weak. The converse is false. Wikipedia’s “lumpable iff exit rates constant on each block” is the **strong** test; it is not the weak test.

On a finite irreducible chain the set of good starts is a union of polyhedral cones invariant under \(P\). Checking that can cost exponential time in the number of states. Your kernel is worse: each sector fiber is **countably infinite** (unbounded \(I\)). The finite-state algorithms do not apply off the shelf.

Stationary \(\pi\) does **not** automatically work. Under \(\pi\), the first-step law \(\mathbb P(Y_1\mid Y_0=q)\) is well-defined as an average. Weak lumpability asks whether that same average is recovered **after every** \(q\)-history that returns to the same sector. If the posterior inside the fiber has moved, the exit law moves with it.

Same \(q\), different one-step laws. That kills strong lumpability. For weak lumpability those two numbers are allowed to differ, provided every reachable posterior on \(\{q=000\}\) produces the **same mixture**.

The engineering start in the spec does **not** look like a good candidate. Chain \(i\) begins at the Dirac

\[
M=0,\quad I=\Gamma q^{(i)}.
\]

That is a single point in the fiber, not \(\pi(\cdot\mid q)\). From \(q=000\) you start at \(I=0\), so the first-step flip rate is the small number \(0.00281\). After a successful unit-cycle out and back, you are typically on \(\lvert W_x\rvert=0\) or \(2\) with different Bessel weights. The posterior is no longer that Dirac. So the eight-chain profile is the situation weak lumpability is designed to reject: two visits to the same label, two different hidden conditionals.

## Why \(\pi\) is also in trouble

A first-step average under \(\pi(\cdot\mid q=000)\) exists. Call it \(\bar p\). Weak lumpability needs every later visit to \(000\) to see that same \(\bar p\).

Two \(q\)-histories that end at \(000\):

1. **Long sojourn.** No flip of bit \(x\) for many microticks.  
   Unit-cycle proposals on \(x\) were either not drawn or rejected. Rejection is more likely when the reference-cycle currents are near \(0\) (\(R_\pm\approx 0.20\)) than when \(\lvert I\rvert=2\) (downhill accept \(=1\)).  
   The sojourn **updates the posterior toward small \(\lvert I\rvert\) on \(\Gamma_x\)**. Expected next flip rate drops below \(\bar p\).

2. **Out and back.** \(000\to e_x\to 000\).  
   Each observed flip is a *accepted* unit cycle, which is more likely from a state with large downhill \(R\).  
   The posterior after the return is weighted toward those high-accept states. Expected next flip rate sits above the sojourn value.

Even-cycle moves (\(\Delta W=\pm 2\), \(\Delta q=0\)) run in the background and are invisible to \(q\). They move \(\lvert W\rvert\) inside the sector. The \(q\)-path does not record that motion, so it cannot pin the posterior to \(\pi(\cdot\mid q)\).

That is an obstruction, not a finished invariant proof. It is the same mechanism as the \(4/5\) vs \(1/5\) hidden-\(A\) example in the v1.6 receipt: one label, two internals, and the label history reweights which internal you are in.

## Higher-order weak lumpability

Geiger–Temmel: the lumped process may fail to be order-\(1\) Markov but still be order-\(k\) Markov, either for every start (strong \(k\)-lumpability) or only in stationarity (a weak form).

That is exactly the Twin Timelines fact on the other lane: the slip sequence is **not** Markov of any finite order. Word complexity \(m+1\) says each extra bit of memory still splits. Do not expect “keep last \(k\) sectors” to repair \(q\) on the torus either. Sector memory does not record even-cycle motion or the sign of \(W\).

## What would actually decide it

A finite identity, still cheaper than the 65-million-update pilot:

Fix \(q=000\). Compare estimated or exact

\[
\mathbb P(\text{flip }q_x\mid \text{sojourn of length }n)
\quad\text{vs}\quad
\mathbb P(\text{flip }q_x\mid 000\to e_x\to 000).
\]

If those differ for the target \(\pi\) or for the eight-chain start, weak lumpability of \(q\) fails for that start. If they agree for all reachable histories, it holds for that start only.

Because \(I\) is unbounded you cannot enumerate the fiber. You can still truncate at a current box \(\lvert I_\ell\rvert\le N\), compute the restricted kernel, and watch the two posteriors. That is a bounded validator, not a production mixing claim.

## How this sits in the three contracts

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Strong lumpability of \(q\) | **False** (exact, last turn) |
| Weak lumpability of \(q\) under the eight-chain Dirac start | Almost certainly false; first step already uses the \(I=0\) rate |
| Weak lumpability of \(q\) under \(\pi\) | **Open**, with a concrete obstruction via sojourn vs out-and-back posteriors |
| Weak \(k\)-lumpability for finite sector memory | Unlikely; hidden even-cycle motion is not in the \(q\)-word |
| TOH / Geometry Maximization criterion | Still the **strong** test. Weak is a weaker statistical accident, not an autonomous state |

TOH §4 asked for equal next-observation **laws whenever labels agree**, independent of start. That is strong lumpability. Weak lumpability would only say: “if you happen to start in this special measure, the coarse picture looks Markov.” Your own receipt already refused to treat a special initialization as the universal criterion.

So: \(q\) is not a state. It might be a convenient diagnostic. Do not promote a sector PASS, or a stationary-looking sector table, into predictive closure.

Geiger–Temmel (Temmel, not Tempel) is the paper your v1.6 receipt already points at: lumping can fail as order-1 Markov and still be order-\(k\), or fail at every finite \(k\). That is the right language for Twin Timelines and for \(q\).

## What they actually define

Let \(X_n\) be irreducible and aperiodic on a **finite** space, \(g:X\to Y\) a lumping, \(Y_n=g(X_n)\). Then \(Y\) is a function of a Markov chain — a functional hidden Markov model.

**Strong \(k\)-lumpability.** For every initial law of \(X\), the process \(Y\) is a homogeneous Markov chain of order \(k\): the next label depends on the last \(k\) labels only, and that kernel does not depend on how \(X\) started.

**Weak / stationary \(k\)-lumpability.** The same statement, but only when \(X\) is started in its invariant measure \(\mu\).

Order \(1\) is ordinary strong / weak lumpability. Their Theorem 9 is an entropy test: strong \(k\)-lumpability iff

\[
H(Y_n\mid Y_{n-1},\ldots,Y_{n-k},X_0)
=H(Y_n\mid Y_{n-1},\ldots,Y_{n-k})
\]

almost surely. Knowing the hidden start adds nothing once you already have \(k\) labels. If the equality is strict, the label word is still missing state.

They also separate a different question: **entropy-rate preservation**, \(H(X\mid Y)=0\). That is about whether lumped trajectories determine original trajectories in the large-\(n\) limit. It is not the same as \(k\)-lumpability. A lumping can lose entropy and still be Markov, or preserve entropy and fail to be Markov. Their combinatorial switch is the split-merge index \(K\): if two different hidden paths can share a lumped word and then merge or split, \(K<\infty\) and entropy is lost at a linear rate. If the transition graph is fully positive, \(K=1\) automatically.

Sparse sufficient conditions they use later: single-entry (at most one hidden successor in each lump) and single-forward-\(k\)-sequence. Those are graph properties. Your kernels are not sparse in that sense.

Assumptions that **do not** hold on the torus kernel: finite hidden space. \(I\) is unbounded. So Geiger–Temmel theorems do not apply as stated to \(X=(I,M,q)\). They do apply, in spirit, to any finite truncation, and they apply cleanly to the 39-screen once you discretize phase.

## Twin Timelines is the exact obstruction

Full state: \(\rho_{n+1}=\{\rho_n-\tau\}\). That is deterministic order-1 Markov (in fact a rotation).

Observation: \(\sigma_n=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\tau)}(\rho_n)\).

Twin Timelines: two distinct phases, identical \(\sigma\)-words of length \(39\), different bit \(40\). The construction extends to every finite length. So

\[
\mathbb P(\sigma_{n+1}\mid \sigma_n,\ldots,\sigma_{n-k+1})
\]

is not a function of the last \(k\) bits, for any finite \(k\).

That is **failure of strong \(k\)-lumpability for every \(k\)**. It is also failure of the stationary version: the rotation is uniquely ergodic, so there is only one invariant law on the circle, and the twins still exist.

This is stronger than “not Markov.” It is “not a hidden-Markov lump of any finite memory.” The combinatorial shadow is the same as Sturmian / mechanical words: factor complexity

\[
p(n)=n+1.
\]

Exactly one word of each length has two right extensions. That extra extension is the Twin Timelines bit. A genuine order-\(k\) Markov source on a binary alphabet has complexity eventually bounded by a constant times the number of \(k\)-blocks. Linear complexity rules that out.

Entropy-rate language is almost silent here. Both \(H(X)\) and \(H(Y)\) have rate zero: the whole future is a function of \(\rho_0\). What grows is the **finite-n preimage** — the set of phases that share an \(n\)-word has total length \(\sim 1/(n+1)\). That is why finite history never closes and the infinite itinerary still determines the orbit off a countable set of endpoints. Geiger–Temmel’s \(T_n=|R(Y^n)|\) is exactly that preimage count. On a discretized 39-bin circle it is finite and grows until the mesh sees the twins.

## Sector process \(q_n\)

Hidden state \(X=(I,M,q)\), lump \(g(X)=q\). Finite-state hypotheses fail.

The analog of a length-\(k\) test is still well-posed:

\[
\mathbb P(q_{n+1}\mid q_n,\ldots,q_{n-k+1},X_0)
\stackrel{?}{=}
\mathbb P(q_{n+1}\mid q_n,\ldots,q_{n-k+1}).
\]

We already know \(k=1\) fails strongly. Higher \(k\) would mean: a finite sector word is enough, the hidden \((I,M)\) adds nothing.

It should fail for every fixed \(k\), for the same reason as the sojourn / out-and-back split. A \(k\)-word of \(q\) records only which unit-cycle bits flipped. It does not record

- even-cycle moves (\(\Delta W=\pm 2\)),
- rejected unit-cycle attempts,
- the sign of \(W\),
- currents off the reference cycle.

Those change the Bessel acceptances that set the next flip rate. Extra sector memory does not see them. That is “not SFS\(_k\)”: many hidden successors sit in the same next lump.

Entropy rate is the other Geiger–Temmel axis. Here it almost certainly **is** lost. From a given \(q\)-word, the preimage in \(I\) grows (even cycles wander inside the sector). \(K\) is finite as soon as two currents can share a sector path and then split on the next flip. That is compatible with the pilot: sector occupancy looked fine while signed \(W\) did not mix. Occupancy of lumps is not reconstruction of \(X\), and it is not \(k\)-lumpability.

## What not to mix

| Property | Slip \(\sigma\) | Sector \(q\) |
|---|---|---|
| Strong 1-lumpable | No | No (exact) |
| Strong \(k\)-lumpable, some \(k\) | No, all \(k\) | Open as a theorem; mechanism says no |
| Stationary \(k\)-lumpable | No | Open |
| Entropy rate of the observation | \(0\) (deterministic coding) | Positive on the unbounded kernel |
| Entropy-rate preservation \(H(X\mid Y)=0\) | Trivial rates; finite words do not determine \(\rho\) | No: \(I\) is not a function of the \(q\)-path |
| Predictive closure in TOH §4 | Fails | Fails |

TOH asked for autonomous next-observation laws. That is strong \(1\)-lumpability. Geiger–Temmel’s upgrade is only: maybe \(k\) labels suffice even if one does not. Twin Timelines says the 39-screen observation does not get there at any \(k\). The torus sector label should be treated the same way until someone produces a \(k\) for which the hidden \((I,M)\) is independent of the next bit given that word.

## A test that stays inside the packet

You do not need the full GT linear-algebra machine.

- **39-screen.** Already done: complexity \(n+1\) and the explicit twins.
- **Torus.** On a current box \(\lvert I_\ell\rvert\le N\), the hidden space is finite, GT applies, and you can compute whether \(Y=q\) is HMC\(_k\) for small \(k\) by comparing next-bit frequencies across all hidden states that share the same last \(k\) sectors. If any two disagree, strong \(k\)-lumpability fails on that box. Let \(N\) grow. If the disagreement survives, it is not a cutoff artifact.

That is the same cheap identity-check as last time, just with memory \(k\) instead of memory \(0\). It is not a mixing diagnostic and it is not a ToE. It is the precise meaning of “higher-order Markov” for the object you actually sample.
