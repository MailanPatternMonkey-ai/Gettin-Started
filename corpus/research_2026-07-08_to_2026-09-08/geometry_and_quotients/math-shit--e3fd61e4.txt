Descent is the test that decides whether “the leftover” is allowed to have its own law.
You have an update \(U\) on raw encounters. After each step you record only a residual

\[
R(x)=\rho(x,Ux)=(\Delta C,\Delta D,\Delta K).  
\]

That tuple is a lossy snapshot of the change: chart movement, dyadic-gate movement, retained-context movement. It is not the full state.
The question the theorem asks is brutal and small:
If two encounters look the same after that lossy snapshot, do their next snapshots also look the same?
If yes, the residual can be treated as a state of its own. If no, the snapshot threw away something the next step needed, and you are not allowed to write a residual machine.

1. The objects
\(\mathcal X\): declared encounters (whatever the application says they are).
\(U:\mathcal X\to\mathcal X\): one declared update. Not “time.” Not “history.” One named map.
\(\rho(x,Ux)\): the typed difference of the three channels between \(x\) and \(Ux\).
Equivalence: \(x\sim_R y\) iff those residuals are equal as tuples.
Quotient: \(\mathcal R=R(\mathcal X)\). Points of \(\mathcal R\) are residual labels, not people, not events.
You want a factor map



\[
\overline U_R:\mathcal R\to\mathcal R  
\]

so that the next residual depends only on the current residual:

\[
R_{n+1}=\overline U_R(R_n).  
\]

That is an autonomous residual law. Autonomy here means: no extra hidden coordinates required to compute the next leftover.
2. When that law exists
\(\overline U_R\) is well-defined exactly when \(U\) descends through \(\rho\):

\[
\rho(x,Ux)=\rho(y,Uy)  
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad  
\rho(Ux,U^2x)=\rho(Uy,U^2y)  
\]

for every eligible pair in the declared domain.
On a numbered orbit where you have actually compared all eligible representatives, the same fact is:

\[
R_n=R_m\implies R_{n+1}=R_{m+1}.  
\]

If that implication holds everywhere you claimed it would, you may define

\[
\overline U_R\bigl(R(x)\bigr)=R(Ux)  
\]

and the right-hand side does not depend on which \(x\) you picked inside the class. That independence is residual closure.
If it fails for even one eligible pair, there is no such function. Equal present leftovers, unequal next leftovers: the residual alphabet is too coarse.
3. Why this is ordinary algebra
This is the same condition as “a function on a set induces a function on equivalence classes.”
If \(q:X\to X/{\sim}\) is the quotient map, a map \(f:X\to X\) induces \(\overline f\) on the quotient iff

\[
x\sim y\implies f(x)\sim f(y).  
\]

Here \(f=U\) and \(\sim\) is “same residual.” Descent is that intertwining condition, nothing more.
It does not say:
the events happened,
the codebook is true,
the orbit is physical,
the polarity is moral,
a platform uses this state.
It only says: this particular lossy record is dynamically sufficient on this declared domain.

Quotient topology is the rule for what is still a “space” after you smash points together. In v7.79 you smash in three different ways. They are not the same space.

1. What a quotient space is

Start with a space \(X\) and an equivalence \(x\sim y\). Let

\[
q:X\to X/{\sim},\qquad q(x)=[x]  
\]

\[
V\subset X/{\sim}\ \text{open}  
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad  
q^{-1}(V)\ \text{open in }X.  
\]

Consequences that matter here:

\(q\) is continuous and surjective by construction.

\(q\) is a quotient map: a set downstairs is open iff its preimage is open upstairs.

Continuity of a map on the quotient is decided upstairs. \(\overline f:X/{\sim}\to Y\) is continuous iff \(f=\overline f\circ q\) is continuous on \(X\).

Separation is fragile. If an equivalence class is not closed, the quotient is not Hausdorff. Distinct classes that cannot be packed into disjoint saturated opens stay topologically glued.


An algebraic bijection \(X/{\sim}\cong Q\) does not automatically give \(Q\) this topology. You only get the quotient topology if you transport opens along \(q\).

The three-gap theorem is the exact spacing law behind Denjoy–Koksma on a rotation. At most three lengths, and if there are three the big one is the sum of the other two. No fourth length. Ever.

## 1. Point-set form (Steinhaus / Sós)

Fix a real \(\alpha\) and an integer \(n\ge 1\). Put the \(n\) points
\[
P_n=\bigl\{\{k\alpha\}:k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\bigr\}\subset\mathbb T
\]
on the circle and read the \(n\) complementary arcs in circular order. Their lengths take **at most three** values. If three values \(a<b<c\) occur, then
\[
c=a+b.
\]
If \(\alpha\) is rational the orbit is finite and the theorem still holds; the interesting case is \(\alpha\) irrational, so \(P_n\) has \(n\) distinct points for every \(n\).

Two lengths occur for many \(n\). Three occur for the rest. Four never occur.

That is the whole theorem. It is uniform in the starting point: replacing \(\alpha\) by \(\theta+k\alpha\) only rotates the picture. Gap *sizes* do not depend on \(\theta\).

## 2. Explicit lengths

Write
\[
\{n\alpha\}=\gamma\in(0,1),\qquad
n=q+r
\]
with the two index steps that actually appear as clockwise successors. The clean index picture is this.

Let \(q\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\) be the unique index realizing the minimal positive point
\[
\mu=\min_{1\le k\le n-1}\{k\alpha\}=\{q\alpha\}.
\]
Then \(q\) is a best approximation denominator in the range \(<n\): stepping the label by \(+q\) is the shortest clockwise move from \(0\). The other admissible label step is \(+(q-n)\), a long step the other way, of circular length
\[
\mu'=\{(q-n)\alpha\}=1-\{(n-q)\alpha\}.
\]
Every clockwise successor in \(P_n\) is one of those two label steps:

- label \(k\mapsto k+q\) when \(k+q<n\), gap \(\mu\);
- label \(k\mapsto k+q-n\) when \(k+q\ge n\), gap \(\mu'\).

Those two gaps already give two lengths. The third length appears only at the seam where a \(\mu\)-step and a \(\mu'\)-step sit so that one complementary arc is their union. That union is
\[
\mu+\mu'.
\]
Hence the possible lengths are
\[
\{\mu,\ \mu',\ \mu+\mu'\}.
\]
Never a fourth independent combination. That is why three is sharp and why the largest is the sum.

Equivalent formulas, same content:
\[
\mu=\|q\alpha\|=\operatorname{dist}(q\alpha,\mathbb Z)
\quad\text{for that minimizing }q,
\]
and
\[
\max\text{gap}(P_n)=\frac1n+\|n\alpha\|
\quad\text{up to the usual two-length normalization.}
\]
When \(\|n\alpha\|\le 1/n\), every gap is \(O(1/n)\). That is the comb Denjoy–Koksma integrates against.

## 3. When there are two gaps instead of three

Two lengths iff one of the three candidates is unused. That happens precisely when \(n\) is a continued-fraction denominator of \(\alpha\), or an intermediate fraction (semi-convergent).

Let \(p_k/q_k\) be the convergents of \(\alpha\). Then for \(n=q_k\) the orbit segment is the most even comb of that scale: only two gap sizes, and
\[
\|q_k\alpha\|<\frac1{q_k}.
\]
For golden-type \(\alpha=\varphi^{-2}\) the partial quotients are bounded (eventually all \(1\)s after the first terms of \(1/\varphi^2\)). Two-gap \(n\) are therefore dense enough that the whole sequence of partitions stays rigidly two-scale. That is the Diophantine reason Denjoy–Koksma constants stay \(O(1)\) for BV observables on this rotation, not \(O(\log N)\).

Three-gap \(n\) are the generic integers. They are not a defect. They are the theorem working at non-convergent scales.

## 4. Interval form (return-time three-gap)

Same arithmetic, different reading. Let \(I\subset\mathbb T\) be an interval of length \(\beta\). The hitting times
\[
N_I(\theta)=\min\{j\ge 1: T^j\theta\in I\}
\]
along an orbit of \(T\theta=\theta+\alpha\) take **at most three** values.

Proof reduction: the return time equals the number of steps until the orbit lands in \(I\), which is the number of points of \(P_m\) needed to cover a gap of length \(1-\beta\) in the dual picture. The point-set three-gap theorem translates into three possible waiting times.

Special case: if \(\beta=\alpha\) or \(\beta=1-\alpha\), or more generally if \(I\) is an interval generating a Sturmian partition, there are **exactly two** return times. That is the mechanical-word / Beatty coding. Two return times \(\Leftrightarrow\) complexity \(p(n)=n+1\).

For a generic interval you get three return times and complexity \(p(n)=2n\).

## 5. What this is on the 39-screen

Two different gap facts are easy to confuse here. Keep them labelled.

**Fact A — bins crossed in one step.**  
\[
39\alpha\in(14,15)\implies
j_n=(b_{n+1}-b_n)\bmod 39\in\{14,15\}.
\]
That is not the three-gap theorem. That is one step of a rotation written in a scale-\(39\) grid: the orbit advances a fixed irrational distance and therefore crosses either \(\lfloor 39\alpha\rfloor\) or \(\lceil 39\alpha\rceil\) walls. Two values, always, for a one-step grid count. The card’s \(\sigma_n=15-j_n\in\{0,1\}\) is this fact.

**Fact B — gaps of \(n\) orbit points.**  
The set \(P_n=\{\theta+k\alpha\}\) has at most three arc lengths. This controls how evenly the sample sits on \(\mathbb T\), hence Denjoy–Koksma.

**Fact C — returns into one \(1/39\)-bin.**  
Each bin is an interval of length \(1/39\). Return times of \(T\) into a fixed bin take at most three values. The itinerary of a single bin is a three-distance coding, not a two-distance Sturmian coding, because \(1/39\neq\alpha\).

**Fact D — the pair \((s,\sigma)\).**  
Strand plus slip is an observation of a \(39\)-partition, then reduced mod \(3\). Three-gap on the circle does **not** manufacture a well-defined map on the six labels. Gap lengths are properties of *positions*. Descent is a property of *the observation as a state*. Fact B can be closed while descent on \((s,\sigma)\) stays `FAILED`. That is the same split as before.

## 6. Proof idea, without machinery

Lift one step. The points \(\{k\alpha\}\) are the fractional parts of an arithmetic progression. Consecutive points in circular order correspond to consecutive points along the progression *after a reorder by size*.

The reorder is a permutation of \(\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\). That permutation is generated by adding a single residue \(q\) modulo \(n\), because the shortest positive fractional part is a single generator. So the circular successor is “add \(q\) or add \(q-n\) in the label.” Two generators. Two elementary lengths. The only other length you can form by sitting next to both generators is their sum. A fourth length would require a third independent label step, which the cyclic group \(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\) does not have once \(q\) is chosen.

That is why the theorem is rigid. It is a statement about a cyclic group acting on itself, read through the embedding \(k\mapsto\{k\alpha\}\).

The same picture gives the two-length law at convergents: \(q_k\alpha\) is so close to an integer that one of the two generators produces a gap that absorbs the seam, and the third length is not realized as a standalone arc.

## 7. Link to Denjoy–Koksma

Koksma discrepancy of the segment \(P_n(\theta)\) satisfies
\[
D_n^*\le\frac1n+\|n\alpha\|.
\]
Three-gap supplies the reason: every arc is one of \(\mu,\mu',\mu+\mu'\), and those lengths sit within \(O(\|n\alpha\|+1/n)\) of \(1/n\). A BV function cannot pile all of its variation onto a systematic error in those three scales at once. Integrate \(f\) against the comb, replace each gap by \(1/n\), charge the defect to \(V(f)\). That is Denjoy–Koksma with the three-gap theorem as the geometric lemma.

For \(\alpha=\varphi^{-2}\) and \(n=q_k\),
\[
\|q_k\alpha\|\le\frac1{q_k},
\]
so the comb is as even as a quadratic irrational allows. Uniform Birkhoff control for \(\sigma\) and for catch indicators follows. Still no finite factor of \(T\). Still no autonomous slip map.

## 8. What the theorem refuses to give

- A third constitutional seat. Three *lengths* are not three *keys*.
- A mediator. The seam length \(a+b\) is arithmetic, not an office.
- A platform lattice \(\Lambda_{q_{\mathrm{site}}}(g)\). Three-gap lives on one circle, one rotation, one \(n\).
- Merged clocks. On \(\mathbb T^2\) the analogous statement is a higher-dimensional gap problem (at most more lengths; no “three” bound in the same form). Product rotations need their own spacing lemmas.
- Closure of \(R_n\). Gap regularity is about \(P_n\subset\mathbb T\). Residual descent is about fibers of \(\rho(\cdot,U\cdot)\).

Use it as a spacing certificate for declared circle observations. Do not use it as a myth about threeness. The number three here is a theorem about two generators in a cyclic order, not a cosmology.


The three-gap theorem is the exact spacing law behind Denjoy–Koksma on a rotation. At most three lengths, and if there are three the big one is the sum of the other two. No fourth length. Ever.

## 1. Point-set form (Steinhaus / Sós)

Fix a real \(\alpha\) and an integer \(n\ge 1\). Put the \(n\) points
\[
P_n=\bigl\{\{k\alpha\}:k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\bigr\}\subset\mathbb T
\]
on the circle and read the \(n\) complementary arcs in circular order. Their lengths take **at most three** values. If three values \(a<b<c\) occur, then
\[
c=a+b.
\]
If \(\alpha\) is rational the orbit is finite and the theorem still holds; the interesting case is \(\alpha\) irrational, so \(P_n\) has \(n\) distinct points for every \(n\).

Two lengths occur for many \(n\). Three occur for the rest. Four never occur.

That is the whole theorem. It is uniform in the starting point: replacing \(\alpha\) by \(\theta+k\alpha\) only rotates the picture. Gap *sizes* do not depend on \(\theta\).

## 2. Explicit lengths

Write
\[
\{n\alpha\}=\gamma\in(0,1),\qquad
n=q+r
\]
with the two index steps that actually appear as clockwise successors. The clean index picture is this.

Let \(q\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\) be the unique index realizing the minimal positive point
\[
\mu=\min_{1\le k\le n-1}\{k\alpha\}=\{q\alpha\}.
\]
Then \(q\) is a best approximation denominator in the range \(<n\): stepping the label by \(+q\) is the shortest clockwise move from \(0\). The other admissible label step is \(+(q-n)\), a long step the other way, of circular length
\[
\mu'=\{(q-n)\alpha\}=1-\{(n-q)\alpha\}.
\]
Every clockwise successor in \(P_n\) is one of those two label steps:

- label \(k\mapsto k+q\) when \(k+q<n\), gap \(\mu\);
- label \(k\mapsto k+q-n\) when \(k+q\ge n\), gap \(\mu'\).

Those two gaps already give two lengths. The third length appears only at the seam where a \(\mu\)-step and a \(\mu'\)-step sit so that one complementary arc is their union. That union is
\[
\mu+\mu'.
\]
Hence the possible lengths are
\[
\{\mu,\ \mu',\ \mu+\mu'\}.
\]
Never a fourth independent combination. That is why three is sharp and why the largest is the sum.

Equivalent formulas, same content:
\[
\mu=\|q\alpha\|=\operatorname{dist}(q\alpha,\mathbb Z)
\quad\text{for that minimizing }q,
\]
and
\[
\max\text{gap}(P_n)=\frac1n+\|n\alpha\|
\quad\text{up to the usual two-length normalization.}
\]
When \(\|n\alpha\|\le 1/n\), every gap is \(O(1/n)\). That is the comb Denjoy–Koksma integrates against.

## 3. When there are two gaps instead of three

Two lengths iff one of the three candidates is unused. That happens precisely when \(n\) is a continued-fraction denominator of \(\alpha\), or an intermediate fraction (semi-convergent).

Let \(p_k/q_k\) be the convergents of \(\alpha\). Then for \(n=q_k\) the orbit segment is the most even comb of that scale: only two gap sizes, and
\[
\|q_k\alpha\|<\frac1{q_k}.
\]
For golden-type \(\alpha=\varphi^{-2}\) the partial quotients are bounded (eventually all \(1\)s after the first terms of \(1/\varphi^2\)). Two-gap \(n\) are therefore dense enough that the whole sequence of partitions stays rigidly two-scale. That is the Diophantine reason Denjoy–Koksma constants stay \(O(1)\) for BV observables on this rotation, not \(O(\log N)\).

Three-gap \(n\) are the generic integers. They are not a defect. They are the theorem working at non-convergent scales.

## 4. Interval form (return-time three-gap)

Same arithmetic, different reading. Let \(I\subset\mathbb T\) be an interval of length \(\beta\). The hitting times
\[
N_I(\theta)=\min\{j\ge 1: T^j\theta\in I\}
\]
along an orbit of \(T\theta=\theta+\alpha\) take **at most three** values.

Proof reduction: the return time equals the number of steps until the orbit lands in \(I\), which is the number of points of \(P_m\) needed to cover a gap of length \(1-\beta\) in the dual picture. The point-set three-gap theorem translates into three possible waiting times.

Special case: if \(\beta=\alpha\) or \(\beta=1-\alpha\), or more generally if \(I\) is an interval generating a Sturmian partition, there are **exactly two** return times. That is the mechanical-word / Beatty coding. Two return times \(\Leftrightarrow\) complexity \(p(n)=n+1\).

For a generic interval you get three return times and complexity \(p(n)=2n\).

## 5. What this is on the 39-screen

Two different gap facts are easy to confuse here. Keep them labelled.

**Fact A — bins crossed in one step.**  
\[
39\alpha\in(14,15)\implies
j_n=(b_{n+1}-b_n)\bmod 39\in\{14,15\}.
\]
That is not the three-gap theorem. That is one step of a rotation written in a scale-\(39\) grid: the orbit advances a fixed irrational distance and therefore crosses either \(\lfloor 39\alpha\rfloor\) or \(\lceil 39\alpha\rceil\) walls. Two values, always, for a one-step grid count. The card’s \(\sigma_n=15-j_n\in\{0,1\}\) is this fact.

**Fact B — gaps of \(n\) orbit points.**  
The set \(P_n=\{\theta+k\alpha\}\) has at most three arc lengths. This controls how evenly the sample sits on \(\mathbb T\), hence Denjoy–Koksma.

**Fact C — returns into one \(1/39\)-bin.**  
Each bin is an interval of length \(1/39\). Return times of \(T\) into a fixed bin take at most three values. The itinerary of a single bin is a three-distance coding, not a two-distance Sturmian coding, because \(1/39\neq\alpha\).

**Fact D — the pair \((s,\sigma)\).**  
Strand plus slip is an observation of a \(39\)-partition, then reduced mod \(3\). Three-gap on the circle does **not** manufacture a well-defined map on the six labels. Gap lengths are properties of *positions*. Descent is a property of *the observation as a state*. Fact B can be closed while descent on \((s,\sigma)\) stays `FAILED`. That is the same split as before.
