The arithmetic explains why

Three 364-day years are:

\[
3(364)=1092\text{ days}.
\]

A triennial scheme contains 37 lunar months because an intercalary month gets inserted.

But 37 ideal 29.5-day lunations are:

\[
37(29.5)=1091.5.
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
1092-1091.5=0.5\text{ day}.
}
\]

There's your seam.

If you let the lunar phase engine run continuously, it arrives at the next three-year boundary half a day early.

If instead you say:

> “New three-year table. Start again from the original indexed dates.”



you erase that accumulated half-day.

That is exactly why Hamernik and Ratzon calculate that a continuously propagated 29.5-day dwqh would shift to approximately I/16 in year four, and then II/16, whereas the actual manuscript resets to I/17 and II/17, reproducing year one. 

Over six solar years the difference becomes even prettier:

\[
6(364)=2184
\]

while

\[
74(29.5)=2183.
\]

So a continuously running 29.5-day astronomical process and two duplicated 1092-day triennial tables differ by:

\[
\boxed{1\text{ full day}}
\]

after six years.

That's my arithmetic from their reconstructed system, not a separate manuscript claim.

The most important methodological gain is Full Thaw II:

\[
\boxed{\text{artifact} \neq \text{interpretation} \neq \text{model agreement}}
