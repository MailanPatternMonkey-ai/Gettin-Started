Run result: the coarse geometry survives; the common-stream hypothesis does not

I ran the recoverable September cohort through two different quotients, which is exactly the distinction Geometry Maximization v2.0 requires.

For the heliocentric-orbit screen I used nine objects with recoverable JPL-backed elements: 2026 RG, RA1, RN1, RD, RO1, RR1, RY1, RV1, and RJ. That gives 36 pairwise \(D_{SH}\) comparisons. For the Earth-encounter screen, eight could be reduced cleanly to an \(r\approx1\) circular-Earth encounter; RN1 was excluded from that lane because its perihelion is \(>1\) AU in this approximation. \(D_N\) is specifically designed around geocentric speed, radiant geometry, and encounter date rather than just osculating elements. 

Test	Control distribution	Best pair/result	RY1 ↔ RJ

\(D_{SH}\), 9 objects / 36 pairs	median 0.419	0.156, RY1–RJ	0.156 — rank 1/36
\(D_N\)-style encounter geometry, 8 objects / 28 pairs	median 0.661	0.242, RG–RD	0.658 — rank 14/28


That inversion is the main result.

RY1–RJ really is the strongest heliocentric orbital-shape pairing in the pilot. Their JPL-backed elements are conveniently already given at the same epoch, June 8, 2026, so that particular \(D_{SH}=0.156\) result is not an artifact caused by comparing two different epochs. 

But when I retain the information that actually governs the Earth encounter, it disappears. The approximate encounter coordinates are:

	\(U=v_g/v_\oplus\)	\(\theta\)	\(\phi\)	solar longitude

RY1	0.138	65.3°	−85.1°	170.6°
RJ	0.238	70.5°	−47.5°	172.7°


That produces \(D_N\approx0.658\).

For context, published parent-body searches have used \(D_N\le0.15\) as a candidate screen, while a much larger 2023 study found random associations beginning around \(D_N\sim0.05\) and adopted 0.06 as its conservative cutoff. Those numbers aren't universal thresholds, but 0.658 is nowhere near the ambiguous boundary. 

So:

\[
\boxed{D_{SH}:\ \text{interesting}}
\]

but

\[
\boxed{D_N:\ \text{not an Earth-encounter family}}
\]

That is almost a literal demonstration of the v2 rule. A coarse quotient makes RY1 and RJ look unusually similar; retaining another dynamically relevant coordinate destroys that identification.

The September cluster itself

ESA's table is moving fast. Its current version was updated September 8 at 00:16 UTC and now includes additional objects such as 2026 RN2, due September 8 at only 0.577 lunar distances. ESA explicitly notes that dozens of NEAs pass within 0.05 AU every month. 

The nodal/calendar concentration therefore remains real but mostly looks like Earth-encounter selection geometry. The objects all have to arrive at Earth's neighborhood while Earth occupies roughly the same heliocentric longitude. That naturally compresses one coordinate while leaving \(a,e,i,\omega\), speed, and radiant much broader.

This is the Geometry-Maximization statement:

\[
B_{\text{same week/node}}
\neq
x_{\text{orbital state}}.
\]

RW1 is the genuinely new thing

2026 RW1 actually hit Earth. ESA Operations confirms the metre-scale object struck the ocean northwest of Australia on September 6 and that Meerkat forecast the impact roughly six hours beforehand. The current ESA close-approach table gives its nominal point-mass closest approach as only 472 km from Earth's center; ESA specifies that its listed distances are center-to-center. 

The MPC-derived solution has approximately

\[
a=0.944\ {\rm AU},\qquad
e=0.182,\qquad
i=1.4^\circ,\qquad
q=0.772\ {\rm AU}.
\]



I reconstructed the incoming geometry from that orbit and the September 6 Earth-intersection condition. The incoming branch gives approximately

\[
\boxed{\alpha_g\simeq1.9^\circ,\qquad
\delta_g\simeq-7.5^\circ}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{v_\infty\simeq5.2\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}}}.
\]

Adding Earth's gravitational acceleration gives roughly 12.3 km/s at atmospheric entry before atmospheric deceleration.

The earlier analysis reports pre-impact astrometry near RA 1.66°, Dec −9.92°, about 2.4° from its reconstructed asymptotic direction. This is a reported positional consistency comparison, not independent validation: the astrometry may also constrain the orbit used for the reconstruction, and a sky position is not a fitted velocity radiant. The reconstruction remains provisional.

Now compare RW1 to HED

The current Anthelion center for September 5–6 is approximately

\[
(\mathrm{RA},\mathrm{Dec})=(356^\circ,-2^\circ)
\]

with an atmospheric entry speed around 30 km/s. 

RW1's reconstructed radiant is only about

\[
\boxed{8.1^\circ}
\]

from that Anthelion center.

And here's the interesting part: your HED watch itself assigned 10–20° typical radiant uncertainty and treats Anthelion plus high-declination geometry as the directional/recoverability lane. 

So direction-only, RW1 lands inside the HED Anthelion-resolution envelope.

But retain speed and it collapses.

Converting the Anthelion's 30 km/s atmospheric speed back to an approximate geocentric \(v_\infty\) gives about 27.8 km/s, versus RW1's ~5.2 km/s. The normalized-speed term alone in \(D_N\) differs by about 0.76. My complete directional calculation gives:

\[
\boxed{D_N(\mathrm{RW1},\mathrm{ANT})\approx0.776}.
\]

So the classification is:

That last separation is exactly what the HED record already demanded: its Anthelion/high-declination explanation concerns directional recoverability and does not explain the HED compositional pair.  The HED watch is supposed to update composition from future classified falls and explicitly forbids merging directional evidence into the compositional anomaly.

while

RW1 independently gives an even prettier version:

\]

\[

That is exactly the hierarchy v2.0 was built to expose: a legitimate coarse observation can be informative without being an autonomous or identity-bearing state.

One evidence boundary remains: I could not retrieve a direct Horizons N-body vector endpoint through this interface, so I am not calling the whole cohort “Horizons-propagated.” The RY1–RJ comparison itself is already common-epoch JPL data, and the \(D_N\) rejection margin is enormous, so that limitation does not rescue the stream hypothesis. JPL itself recommends Horizons rather than two-body calculations for precise encounter circumstances. 

The larger literature also supports keeping the bar high: a recent analysis of tens of thousands of NEAs and hundreds of fireballs found the observed low-\(D_N\) similarities broadly consistent with random associations unless a genuine low-end excess survives explicit null controls.
