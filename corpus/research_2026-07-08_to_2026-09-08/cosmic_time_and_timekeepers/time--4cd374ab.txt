The Temple Scroll takes festival structure and turns it into prescriptions.

The sequence includes New Wine, New Oil, and Wood Offering; New Wine follows the wheat festival by fifty days, New Oil follows fifty days later, with detailed sacrificial and consumption rules. 

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{calendar coordinate}
\rightarrow
\text{festival identity}
\rightarrow
\text{sacrifice / permitted use / cultic action}.
}
\]

That's exactly an authorization bridge.

So:

\[
11QT:
R\rightarrow C\rightarrow Q.
\]

And textual \(J\) is also strong because the work presents an ideal cultic/legal order.

But the Israel Museum itself notes that the Temple Scroll's social provenance remains debated, with scholars differing over its relation to the Qumran community and priestly circles. 

Therefore we absolutely cannot upgrade:

\[
\text{Temple Scroll says}
\]

to:

\[
\text{Jerusalem Temple did}.
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
I_{\text{Jerusalem}}=?
}
\]

That's a major blank edge.

6. ALD gives us a different kind of consequence

Aramaic Levi is even cleaner operationally.

The instructions sequence:

purification,

priestly clothing,

washing,

inspection of altar wood,

burning,

blood application,

ordered sacrificial portions,

meal/oil/wine.

Hillel Mali's analysis identifies these as a continuous order of morning Temple service and emphasizes their procedural sequencing. 

Thus:

\[
R=
\text{ritual state}
\]

\[
C=
\text{if state }x,\text{ perform }y
\]

\[
Q=
\text{correct next action / acceptable sacrifice}.
\]

That's not calendar legislation.

It's closer to executable code:

\[
\boxed{
S_1\rightarrow A_1\rightarrow S_2\rightarrow A_2\rightarrow S_3\dots
}
\]

ALD therefore strongly confirms our earlier distinction:

\[
\boxed{
R_{\text{ritual}}
\neq
T_{\text{calendar}}.
}
\]

The priest needs to know how.

The calendar system determines when.

Still no receipt saying the same desk operated both.

7. Mishmarot now occupies a fascinating middle position

The 24-course system is not imaginary Qumran furniture.

Josephus explicitly describes twenty-four priestly courses and says the division remained in existence in his own day; in his Life, he identifies himself by one of the twenty-four courses. 

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{24-course priestly institution}
}
\]

has independent historical support.

Meanwhile the mishmarot documents coordinate priestly courses with months, festivals, and astronomical/lunar information. Recent work on 4Q321 still describes precisely this conjunction of festivals, priestly courses, and luminary data. 

That produces:

\[
\text{real priestly institution}
\quad+\quad
\text{real calendrical protocol}.
\]

But—and this matters enormously—we do not thereby possess:

\[
\boxed{
\text{proof that Jerusalem actually scheduled those courses according to the surviving 364-day mishmarot scheme}.
}
\]

So \(M\) receives:

\[
\boxed{\text{institutional contact: strong}}
\]

but:

\[
\boxed{\text{specific institutional deployment: open}}.
\]

That's one of the cleanest weak joins on the whole board.

Not:

\boxed{

\boxed{

That stops “authority” from swallowing every kind of technical output.

13. Serekh gives us something much closer to implementation

This is the one that jumped out when I actually tested the board.

The Community Rule does not merely theorize membership.

It contains admission procedures, communal conduct, and a graded penal system. The Israel Museum summarizes the text as dealing with admission, communal meals, prayer, cleansing rituals and community life. 

And the preserved penal material specifies actual sanctions: exclusion, temporary penalties, and permanent expulsion for defined offenses. 

So:

\[
R=\text{community rule}
\]

\[
C=\text{offense/member status}\rightarrow\text{penalty}
\]

\[
Q=\text{exclusion / reduction / expulsion}
\]

\[
J=\text{members of the community}.
\]

Already strong.

But then we get 4Q477.

The IAA officially catalogs it as 4Q Rebukes Reported by the Overseer, and Esther Eshel's scholarly publication is specifically devoted to the document. 

The surviving material records rebukes involving named individuals; scholarship has consequently argued that 4Q477 may show disciplinary procedures of the rule texts being put into practice. That last step remains interpretive rather than mathematically compelled by the fragment, but it is a genuinely different evidentiary situation from merely having another rule text. 

So we get the first really interesting candidate for:

\[
\boxed{
R\rightarrow C\rightarrow Q\rightarrow J\rightarrow I
}
\]

with:

\[
I=\text{probable/arguable rather than absolute}.
\]

🔥

That matters.

Because now we have an actual comparator for what an implementation bridge looks like.

And it looks nothing like “two texts share 364.”

17. The clock/government boundary is therefore not one boundary

We can identify three thresholds.

Threshold 1 — Technical consequence

\[
R\rightarrow C.
\]

A representation becomes usable.

Example:

\[
\text{lunar observation}
\rightarrow
\text{fraction/state}.
\]

No government.


---

Threshold 2 — Normative consequence

\[
R\rightarrow C\rightarrow Q.
\]

The coordinate generates a prescribed action or evaluation.

Example:

\[
\text{festival date}
\rightarrow
\text{sacrifice}.
\]

Now we have normativity.

But still not necessarily government.


---

Threshold 3 — Jurisdiction + implementation

\[
R\rightarrow C\rightarrow Q\rightarrow J\rightarrow I.
\]

Someone has standing to apply the consequence to someone else, and we have evidence of it occurring.

That is where social governance appears.

So:

\[
\boxed{
\text{government does not begin when somebody calculates the day}.
}
\]

It begins when:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a consequence is made binding within a functioning jurisdiction}.
}
\]

That is the answer we were digging for.
